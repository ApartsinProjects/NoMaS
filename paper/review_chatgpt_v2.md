Peer Review for Neurocomputing

Manuscript: No Easy Wins: A Contamination-Controlled Benchmark for Evaluating Anomaly Detection and Model Selection

Summary

This paper introduces ADReal, a curated benchmark of 151 tabular and multivariate time-series anomaly-detection tasks, and SPARC, a normal-only model selector that grades candidate detectors using feature-corrupted synthetic observations. The benchmark addresses duplicate contamination, train/test leakage, label ambiguity, trivial anomalies, and prevalence-aware evaluation, while the selection protocol compares methods against both random selection and a strong best-fixed-detector reference. The revised manuscript substantially improves the empirical comparison by reporting classical-25, deep-7, and full-32 candidate pools, adding IREOS and IFOREST-R, explicitly scoping UDR to its stochastic sub-pool, and acknowledging that SPARC and Goswami are not significantly different on the full pool at a matched 1000-point synthetic budget. The common full-32 oracle is now explicitly defined, resolving an important ambiguity in the earlier presentation. Nevertheless, the central conclusion remains stronger than the evidence warrants: SPARC's favorable classical-pool result is modest and nominally significant (0.109 versus 0.130, p=0.03), the claim that every selector deteriorates with deep-pool expansion has exceptions in the new table, and the interpretation of Table 8 as establishing a neighbor-specific selection mechanism is not supported by family-level marginal counts alone. The benchmark and negative findings are potentially valuable contributions, but the statistical analysis, baseline comparability, mechanism interpretation, and remaining numerical and rhetorical inconsistencies require substantial revision before the paper is ready for Neurocomputing. This review is based on the complete supplied HTML, including all tables, figure captions, and references, without external browsing or independent execution of the code.

Strengths

A well-motivated benchmark contribution (Sections 1–3). The paper addresses genuine weaknesses in anomaly-detection evaluation. The grouped splitting protocol, removal of anomalies identical to normals, anomaly deduplication, and explicit construction pipeline are useful contributions. The distinction between detector training, normal-only validation, and labeled test evaluation is especially important for model selection. Tables 1–3 provide concrete evidence that contamination is not merely a hypothetical concern.

The fixed-detector reference is an appropriate demanding baseline (Section 5). A selector should demonstrate that its per-dataset decisions add value beyond deploying a generally strong detector. Regret against the best available detector, together with a best-single-detector reference, makes this question explicit. The revised Table 6 now states that every column uses the full-32 oracle, so the identical classical/full fixed-reference value of 0.130 is mathematically coherent. This resolves the earlier ambiguity; it should not be treated as an outstanding numerical error.

The three-pool comparison is informative (Section 5, Table 6). Evaluating the same applicable selectors on classical, deep-only, and full candidate pools is a substantial improvement over comparing methods with different eligibility sets. The reported deterioration for SPARC, Goswami, entropy, mass-volume, and the agreement methods is an important negative finding. The manuscript also now distinguishes the relatively small gain in oracle performance from the larger effect of deep eligibility on selector behavior.

The UOMS baseline coverage is improved (Section 5, Table 6). The addition of IREOS and IFOREST-R removes a significant completeness concern. Restricting UDR to a stochastic sub-pool can be methodologically appropriate if its stability criterion is not meaningfully defined for deterministic, heterogeneous candidates. It is preferable to report a restricted, faithful evaluation than to force an invalid application merely to fill a table. The remaining issue is how that restricted result is compared and described.

The Goswami and probe-bias analyses are useful additions (Sections 5–7). Matching the number of synthetic observations is a reasonable first fairness control, and reporting p=0.54 on the full pool is preferable to claiming superiority from the lower SPARC mean alone. Table 8 exposes a substantial mismatch between what the probe rewards and which detector families actually win on real anomalies. The OddBench-concentrated benefit and the acknowledgment that hardening favors certain anomaly mechanisms are also important limitations that should remain prominent.

Weaknesses and concerns
1. The headline fixed-detector victory requires stronger statistical qualification and protection against benchmark-level selection effects (Sections 3.5, 5–6; Tables 6 and 6b)

The main positive result is a reduction in mean regret from 0.130 to 0.109 on the classical-25 pool, with a reported paired Wilcoxon p=0.03. This is a potentially meaningful improvement, but it is not sufficiently strong evidence for the repeated conclusion that SPARC is uniquely successful among label-free selectors. The manuscript evaluates multiple methods, three pools, source-specific subgroups, and design variants, yet it does not identify the prespecified primary comparison, the full family of statistical tests, or any multiplicity adjustment. The OddBench result (p=0.021) is likewise a subgroup finding that appears in the same research and analysis process. Both should be treated as nominal results unless the authors can establish an appropriate confirmatory testing framework.

There is also a distinction between the reported effect and the test used to support it. The tables emphasize mean regret, whereas the Wilcoxon signed-rank test does not directly provide an interval for the mean paired difference. Section 3.5 promises median and tail reporting, but the main results do not provide those distributions. The benchmark contains related source collections and derived tasks, so treating all 151 tasks as independent may overstate precision if task-level dependence is substantial. Stochastic detector training and probe generation introduce further uncertainty that is not quantified.

The best fixed detector is also chosen retrospectively using the benchmark's labeled outcomes. This is a legitimate and deliberately demanding best-single-detector reference, but it is not a prospectively selected label-free deployment policy. Conversely, SPARC's 40% corruption fraction, observed-value construction, and classical-candidate restriction may have benefited from development on the same benchmark. The paper should disclose how these choices were made and distinguish method-development performance from evidence of generalization to new tasks.

Concrete fix: Define a primary comparison family and report all relevant paired p-values, multiplicity-adjusted results where appropriate, paired mean differences with confidence intervals, medians, upper-tail regret, and win/tie/loss counts. Include repeated stochastic runs or justify the fixed-seed protocol, and use source-aware resampling or grouped evaluation where tasks share origins. A complementary leave-one-source-out or nested task-level analysis would be valuable for evaluating generalization of both the fixed policy and SPARC's design choices. If these analyses are not feasible, retain the result but describe it as a modest, nominally significant classical-pool advantage rather than a decisive general victory.

2. The revised Table 6 contradicts the universal pool-expansion claim (Section 5, Table 6, Figure 6)

The new rows make the baseline comparison more complete, but they also reveal that the statement “adding deep detectors raises every label-free selector's regret” is not literally correct. IREOS increases from 0.233 to 0.240, whereas IFOREST-R remains at 0.241 in both the classical and full pools. The unchanged IFOREST-R result is unsurprising if the method always selects the same iForest reference, but it is nevertheless an exception to “every.” Moreover, IREOS performs better on the deep-only pool (0.218) than on the classical pool (0.233), contradicting the additional statement that every method performs best on the classical pool. UDR is not evaluated on these three pools and therefore cannot be included in a universal three-pool claim.

For the nine methods actually displayed across all three pools, eight have higher mean regret on the full pool than on the classical pool, while IFOREST-R is unchanged. This is a strong descriptive finding, but the text should report it accurately. The deep-only interpretation also needs precision: several methods have lower regret than the deep-only random reference of 0.223, including SPARC (0.173), Goswami (0.169), entropy (0.196), mass-volume (0.188), and IREOS (0.218). Thus the deep-only pool is not uniformly worse than random. The revised wording that “several selectors” are below the random floor is better, but “below” is potentially ambiguous because lower regret is better.

The common full-32 oracle is now a defensible convention and should be retained. It means that every selector is measured against the same attainable performance ceiling, while eligibility changes. However, the manuscript should make clear that deep-only regret includes the cost of restricting the candidate set; it is not solely a measure of selection error relative to the best deep detector.

Concrete fix: Replace the universal claim with the exact result: eight of nine displayed matched-pool methods have higher mean regret after expansion, and one is unchanged; IREOS is an exception to the claim that classical-only eligibility is always best. Report the paired expansion differences and their individual p-values for all applicable methods. Distinguish performance relative to the common full oracle from within-pool selection quality, potentially by adding pool-specific regret as a secondary analysis. Correct Figure 6 and its caption accordingly, and use “worse than random” or “higher regret than random” rather than ambiguous floor terminology.

3. The UOMS baseline treatment is now substantially more complete, but UDR needs a properly matched restricted comparison (Section 5, Tables 4 and 6)

The inclusion of IREOS and IFOREST-R addresses the previous omission. Their three-pool results should be treated as part of the main comparison rather than as an afterthought. In particular, IREOS's deep-only result is informative and should not be obscured by a general statement that the reference-based methods all sit above random. The new UDR treatment is reasonable in principle: a stability-based method designed for stochastic configurations should not necessarily be applied to deterministic heterogeneous detectors, where perfect repeatability may be uninformative. However, the manuscript does not explain the UDR sub-pool sufficiently to establish that the implementation is faithful or that the reported regret of 0.238 is a fair comparative result.

Table 4 lists only one default Isolation Forest configuration in the main 32-detector pool. It is therefore unclear which additional stochastic iForest configurations form the UDR sub-pool, how many are included, whether they are part of the main benchmark, and how their training and evaluation budgets compare with the other candidates. A sub-pool-only method can legitimately be evaluated against the common full oracle, but its regret contains both selection error and the restriction imposed by the sub-pool. Comparing 0.238 directly with the full-pool random regret of 0.219 or the best-fixed value of 0.130 does not isolate UDR's ability to select among the candidates for which it was designed.

The normal-only protocol may also require adaptations of IREOS and other internal criteria originally evaluated on contaminated or transductive data. Such adaptations should be specified rather than treating the names of the methods as sufficient evidence of faithful implementation. The paper's conclusion should apply to the tested normal-only adaptations, not necessarily to every published use of these methods.

Concrete fix: Add a separate UDR sub-pool table identifying all configurations, the stability statistic, repetitions, hyperparameters, and candidate count. Report UDR alongside random selection, the best fixed detector, and preferably the other applicable selectors on that same sub-pool, using both common-full-oracle and sub-pool-oracle regret. Explain exactly how IREOS and IFOREST-R are implemented under normal-only validation, including any deviations from their cited definitions. State that UDR is a restricted supplementary comparison and exclude it from universal claims about the three main pools.

4. The matched 1000-point budget is a useful fairness control, but p=0.54 does not establish equivalence or equal robustness (Sections 5–6)

The revised comparison with Goswami is more convincing than an unmatched synthetic-budget comparison. SPARC uses 1000 probe observations, matching the total of five 200-point injection sets. This controls one important resource dimension. It does not establish equality of computational cost, synthetic diversity, variance, or information content. A single large probe and five smaller injection types are different experimental designs; Borda aggregation may trade bias against variance in ways that are not captured by total sample count. The manuscript does not provide enough detail about the exact Goswami implementation, injection strengths, adaptation to flattened time-series windows and tabular data, aggregation rule, or end-to-end computational cost.

On the full pool, the reported regrets are 0.138 for SPARC and 0.151 for Goswami, with p=0.54. The correct inference is that the experiment does not detect a statistically significant difference. It does not demonstrate that the methods “perform alike,” are equivalent, or have equal robustness. Robustness would require analysis of sensitivity to probe seeds, injection types, budgets, candidate-pool changes, or dataset shifts. A nonsignificant Wilcoxon result alone cannot establish any of these properties. The paper is justified in dropping an earlier unsupported claim that Borda is more robust, but it should not replace that claim with an unsupported assertion of equivalence. The classical-pool difference of 0.109 versus 0.122 also requires a direct paired SPARC–Goswami comparison before any superiority claim can be made.

Concrete fix: State the exact Goswami adaptation and all injection and aggregation settings. Report end-to-end generation and scoring costs, not only synthetic-point counts. Include paired confidence intervals for SPARC minus Goswami on each pool and repeated-probe variability. A modest budget-sensitivity experiment would strengthen the comparison. Unless a prespecified equivalence or noninferiority margin is tested, replace “statistically indistinguishable” and “perform alike” with “no statistically significant difference was detected at the matched 1000-point budget.”

5. Table 8 demonstrates a strong family-preference mismatch, but not a reliable neighbor-detector selection mechanism (Section 7, Table 8)

Table 8 is a useful diagnostic addition. The counts are internally consistent: both columns sum to 151, and the oracle counts agree with Table 5. The probe ranks a deep detector first on 72 tasks versus 33 deep-oracle tasks, a distribution detector first on only 1 versus 36 distribution-oracle tasks, and a density/neighbor detector first on 78 versus 68 density/neighbor oracles. These data establish that the probe's family preferences differ substantially from the distribution of real-anomaly winners. They do not establish that the probe correctly identifies the neighbor family on the tasks where that family is optimal.

The table reports marginal counts, not a task-level confusion matrix. The 78 neighbor selections and 68 neighbor oracles could overlap on anywhere from zero to 68 tasks based on those counts alone. Thus the table cannot show whether the probe is successful at identifying neighbor-suitable tasks, whether it systematically selects neighbors on tasks where distribution detectors are optimal, or whether its selected neighbor configuration is close to the oracle. Family size is another confounder: the density/neighbor family contains 13 candidates, compared with seven deep and six distribution candidates. The probability that a family supplies the maximum probe grade can depend on the number and variability of its members.

The mechanistic explanation is plausible but over-certain. Resampling feature values from observed marginals preserves individual marginal distributions in expectation, while disrupting dependencies. It does not guarantee that the resulting points are locally sparse or close to the normal manifold. Some corrupted combinations may be grossly implausible or violate deterministic constraints. A deep model's high probe grade is better described as proxy mismatch than as “overfitting” unless the manuscript demonstrates that its training or configuration specifically adapted to the probe. Likewise, the distribution-family result supports a blind spot in the winner distribution, not the claim that every marginal detector has “nothing to read.”

Concrete fix: Add the task-level confusion matrix between probe-winning and oracle-winning families, with tie handling specified. Report conditional selection accuracy and regret by oracle family, including the frequency and cost of choosing neighbors when another family is optimal. Provide an algorithm-level or family-balanced analysis to separate genuine probe preference from family size. A direct comparison of marginal-rarity, dependency-violation, and local-density characteristics of synthetic and real anomalies would strengthen the mechanism claim. Until then, describe Table 8 as evidence of a neighbor-family preference and a distribution-family blind spot, not proof that SPARC is a successful neighbor-detector detector.

6. The benchmark construction is defensible for a hard-anomaly regime, but its detector independence and generality are overstated (Sections 3.1–3.5, 7–8)

The construction pipeline has several useful controls, but the manuscript sometimes describes them more absolutely than the evidence supports. Section 3 explicitly acknowledges that the separability and selection-trivial floors are computed from the detector pool, yet other passages state that the pipeline is detector-free and that no design choice can be tuned to flatter a detector or selector. The detector-independent construction gates and detector-dependent post-scoring filters must be distinguished. A task removed because the available oracle has ap_norm at or below 0.05 is not intrinsically unsolvable; it is unsolved by the specified pool under the specified protocol. Similarly, removing anomalies identical to normals addresses one form of label ambiguity, not all mislabeling.

The hardening filter deliberately removes marginally easy anomalies and changes the evaluation population. This is a legitimate design for a benchmark of residual difficult detection, but it is not a neutral representation of the original anomaly distribution. The paper acknowledges in Section 8 that the resulting benchmark favors local-density anomalies and joint-structure probes. That limitation should be integrated into the central claims rather than used only as a qualification after the results. The source selection is also substantial: 174 candidate tasks are drawn from 1,192 available datasets, and 151 survive. The manuscript does not provide enough detail in the main text to reproduce all eligibility decisions or assess how much the final rankings depend on the post-scoring floors.

The metric is another point requiring precision. The normalization ap_norm = (AP − r)/(1 − r) is prevalence-adjusted, but it does not make AP fully invariant to prevalence. The base-rate cap at 0.5 also changes the test distribution. These are defensible choices, provided the manuscript does not claim that they eliminate all prevalence effects. For time series, flattening windows and evaluating ordinary window-level rankings should be clearly distinguished from event-level anomaly-detection evaluation.

Concrete fix: Separate detector-independent construction from detector-dependent post-scoring filtering throughout the paper. Provide a complete source manifest, exclusion reasons, threshold definitions, and per-stage counts. Include sensitivity analyses on the 173 constructed tasks before the post-scoring floors and, if feasible, a less aggressively hardened variant. Frame ADReal as a curated hard-anomaly benchmark rather than a universally representative benchmark. Narrow absolute contamination claims to the mechanisms actually controlled, and clarify the prevalence-adjustment and window-level evaluation assumptions.

7. The source-specific result is useful, but the “selection helps precisely where needed and costs nothing elsewhere” interpretation is too strong (Sections 5–8, Table 6b, Figure 7)

The revised source breakdown is one of the most informative parts of the paper. On OddBench, SPARC reaches regret 0.145 versus 0.198 for the fixed detector, with nominal p=0.021. On OvrBench the means are 0.133 versus 0.145, and on time series both are reported as 0.038. This supports a concentrated advantage on OddBench and relatively little average benefit in the other two collections. It does not establish equivalence, zero cost, or that selection helps whenever oracle diversity is high. A selector may fail precisely because the oracle varies in ways that normal-only criteria cannot predict. Table 8's distribution-family blind spot makes this concern particularly relevant.

The current wording also implies that a fixed detector is near-oracle almost everywhere in OvrBench and time series, but the table reports only source-level means. It does not show whether SPARC incurs large losses on a minority of tasks or whether its gains and losses cancel. The OddBench subgroup result should be presented as exploratory unless it was prespecified and tested within an appropriate statistical framework.

Concrete fix: Report source-level paired confidence intervals, win/tie/loss counts, medians, and tail regret. Show the number of tasks on which SPARC materially improves or degrades performance relative to the fixed detector. Analyze whether oracle diversity, normal-data characteristics, or anomaly mechanism predicts selection benefit, preferably using held-out source groups. Replace “costs nothing” and “precisely where” with language about the observed average performance in these collections.

8. SPARC's ablation and implementation details remain insufficient for the strength of the methodological claims (Sections 4 and 6, Table 7)

The method is appealingly simple, and observed-value corruption is a sensible design choice. However, Table 7 contains only the proposed configuration, an interpolation alternative (0.117), and the fixed reference (0.130). It does not provide uncertainty for the 0.008 improvement, nor does it isolate the effect of the corruption fraction, synthetic budget, or candidate restriction. The statement that the observed-value draw “earns the result” is therefore stronger than the ablation demonstrates.

The phrase “one probe and no tuning” also needs qualification. The method has a corruption fraction of approximately 40%, a 1000-point budget, a feature-sampling rule, and a classical-candidate restriction. These may be fixed at deployment, but they are still design choices that could have been selected using benchmark feedback. The paper should distinguish no per-dataset label-based tuning from no research-stage tuning. It should also specify the treatment of categorical, ordinal, missing, and structurally constrained features, and explain how independent feature corruption is applied to flattened time-series windows.

For the detector benchmark, Table 4 provides only broad hyperparameter categories and one configuration per deep model. Common preprocessing is useful but does not establish that all models receive equally effective training or tuning. The family-wide conclusion that deep detectors do not beat classical methods should therefore be restricted to the tested implementations and configurations.

Concrete fix: Provide a compact ablation over corruption fraction, observed-value versus interpolation sampling, probe budget, and candidate eligibility, with paired uncertainty. State how the final design was chosen. Supply exact detector hyperparameter grids, deep architectures, training settings, seeds, stopping rules, and computational budgets. Include a limited sensitivity analysis for important deep-model settings or narrow the conclusions to the specified configurations.

9. Reproducibility and final claim calibration need a submission-ready pass (Sections 1, 5–9; Data and code availability)

The revised manuscript is clearer than the earlier version, but the abstract and conclusion still foreground the classical-pool win without adequately conveying the full-pool limitation. The abstract states that SPARC is the only label-free method that significantly beats the fixed detector, while the main text reports that no label-free method beats the fixed detector on the full pool. The former is defensible only when explicitly restricted to the evaluated methods and the classical-25 candidate set. The conclusion's statement that SPARC succeeds “where it matters” is also more promotional than the evidence supports.

The availability statement says that the benchmark, implementations, and figures are released at the project repository, but the supplied HTML does not provide a usable repository location or permanent archive identifier in that section. A future camera-ready archive does not substitute for reproducibility of the submitted results. The manuscript also needs to make the complete per-task results and statistical scripts available, because the reported p-values, pool-expansion effects, and family-level interpretations cannot be independently verified from aggregate tables alone.

Concrete fix: Revise the abstract and conclusion to state the favorable classical-pool result, the absence of a full-pool fixed-baseline victory, and the source concentration of the benefit in a balanced way. Provide a versioned repository, exact configurations, split manifests, per-task detector scores, selector choices, random seeds, and scripts reproducing every table and statistical test. Replace broad claims about the field with claims about the evaluated methods under the specified normal-only protocol and curated benchmark.

Minor issues

The numerical and presentation audit identifies several corrections and several issues that have already been resolved:

Resolved issues should remain resolved. The common full-32 oracle in Table 6 now explains why the fixed detector is 0.130 in both classical and full columns. The missing IREOS and IFOREST-R rows have been added. The earlier OddBench entropy discrepancy is also corrected: Section 6 and Table 6b now both report 0.164, while time-series entropy and mass-volume are correctly distinguished as 0.145 and 0.155. These should not be cited as outstanding errors.

Table 6b omits eight tasks from the displayed source columns. OvrBench (66), OddBench (39), and TSB-AD (38) sum to 143, while the total is 151. The remaining five ADBench and three DAMI tasks should be shown separately or explicitly identified as omitted columns. The reported totals are not inherently inconsistent, but the table is incomplete as a source breakdown.

Figure 6 should be regenerated or its caption corrected. The caption still states that adding deep detectors raises every selector's regret. It must account for unchanged IFOREST-R and the restricted UDR result. The HTML references an external figure image, so the plotted bars themselves could not be independently checked from the supplied source; the audit covers the table, prose, and caption.

Figure 7 uses “ties” without an equivalence analysis. Equal rounded means on time series do not establish equivalence, and the small OvrBench difference should not be described as zero cost. The caption should report the observed means and statistical evidence precisely.

Table 8 should specify tie handling. Oracle and probe winner counts depend on how equal scores are resolved. The table should also distinguish family-level counts from detector-level selection accuracy. Its totals are correct, but its causal interpretation is not established by those totals.

Section 3.2 and Figure 3 overstate the hardening guarantee. Retained anomalies are not necessarily inseparable by every single feature or direction; they are not flagged by the specified calibrated tests. The distinction matters for both benchmark validity and the proposed probe mechanism.

Section 3.5 should align its statistical description with the reported results. It promises median and tail regret but the principal tables report means. It should specify the estimand, test, confidence intervals, and multiplicity policy rather than suggesting that a difference of means is never reported.

The HTML metadata and footer retain the older title. Both still refer to “Anomaly Detector Model Selection by Normal Manifold Separability,” whereas the displayed manuscript title is “No Easy Wins.” These should be synchronized before submission.

The tone should be more measured. Expressions such as “earns the result,” “the deep detectors are the cause,” “where it matters,” and “audited until each channel is closed” should be replaced with precise statements of the observed evidence and its limitations. The paper's negative findings are sufficiently interesting without categorical language.

Clarify the scope of related work. SPARC is appropriately situated alongside Goswami, SWSA, and DSV. Its novelty should be presented as the particular observed-marginal corruption probe, simple selection rule, and controlled evaluation, rather than synthetic-anomaly model selection as a new general paradigm.

Final recommendation: Major revision

The updated manuscript is materially stronger than a typical first submission. The three-pool analysis, added UOMS baselines, explicit common oracle, matched synthetic budget, and Table 8 diagnostic analysis address important weaknesses and make the negative findings more credible. The paper has a viable contribution for Neurocomputing, particularly as a benchmark and empirical study showing how difficult normal-only model selection remains under a strong fixed-detector reference.

However, the present version is not yet ready for acceptance or minor revision. The main positive result rests on a modest nominal p=0.03 comparison without sufficient treatment of multiplicity, task dependence, or research-stage selection effects. The new Table 6 contradicts the universal pool-expansion claim, UDR's restricted evaluation needs a matched sub-pool reference, and p=0.54 does not support equivalence between SPARC and Goswami. Table 8 establishes a useful probe-bias pattern but not the stronger neighbor-detector mechanism claimed in the discussion. The benchmark's deliberately hardened evaluation regime and the limitations of the finite detector pool also need to be reflected more consistently in the conclusions.

A major revision should focus on reconciling the claims with the complete results, supplying the missing statistical and implementation details, adding a properly contextualized UDR comparison, and strengthening or narrowing the probe-mechanism interpretation. These changes do not necessarily require inventing a new method or rebuilding the benchmark. If the authors present the findings as a carefully bounded empirical contribution rather than a general victory for SPARC, the manuscript could become a useful and publishable study.
